\section{Knowledge Distillation as Multi-Objective Optimization}
\label{sec:kd_moo}

Hinton-style distillation minimises $(1-\lambda)L_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda L_{\mathrm{KD}}$
\citep{hinton2015kd}, which is the linear scalarisation of Section~\ref{sec:moo} with $K=2$.
A training step moves the student along a direction built from the gradients of these losses.
In plain weighted distillation that direction is the weighted sum of the supervised and the
teacher gradients. Two loss gradients conflict when their inner product is negative: their
local first-order preferences point in opposing directions \citep{yu2020pcgrad,zhu2021sckd}.
Conflict is a property of the optimisation state, not of the teacher. A teacher can predict the
correct class with high confidence and still conflict with the label gradient. At temperature 1,
with teacher probabilities $(0.85,0.10,0.05)$, student probabilities $(0.95,0.03,0.02)$ and
label class 1, the cross-entropy logit gradient $p_s-y$ and the forward-KL logit gradient
$p_s-p_t$ have inner product $-0.0077$. This is a local calculation in logit space, not an
experiment, and it says nothing general about parameter-space gradients in every network.
Section~\ref{sec:reliability} covers the separate question of whether the teacher is right; this
section asks how the gradients are combined.

\subsection{How state-of-the-art distillation uses MOO}
\label{sec:kd_moo_methods}

We group the methods by the rule they use: symmetric balancing in the MGDA line, gating and
projection that treat the label loss as primary, and optimiser-side rules that change momentum
or per-sample weights. Table~\ref{tab:kd_moo} summarises the objectives, rule, extra cost and
evidence of each.

\paragraph{AE-KD: MGDA over teachers (lineage).}
AE-KD \citep{du2020aekd} framed ensemble distillation in gradient space. Its
objectives are the per-teacher distillation losses; the cross-entropy term is added outside the
MOO step. It solves a soft-margin version of MGDA whose dual is the min-norm problem with box
constraints $0\le\alpha_m\le C$, and $C\in[1/M,1]$ sets how much disagreement among the $M$
teachers is tolerated. With $C=1$ it is MGDA; smaller $C$ spreads weight across teachers. The
gradients are taken with respect to the student's logits, so the weighting adds little cost. AE-KD
is symmetric among teachers and gives the label no special role. It weighs teachers against each
other, not the task against the teacher. Later multi-teacher methods such as
AGRE-KD \citep{kenfack2025agrekd} use AE-KD as a baseline.

\paragraph{DTO-KD: closed-form min-norm on log losses.}
DTO-KD \citep{hayder2026dtokd} applies FAMO's formulation to distillation. It maximises the worse
of the two relative improvement rates of the task and distillation losses. The dual picks
$\pi^\star=\arg\min_{\pi}\tfrac12\|J\pi\|^2$ with $J=[\nabla\log L_{\mathrm{distill}},\nabla\log
L_{\mathrm{task}}]$, and for two objectives the paper gives the closed form
$\pi_1^\star=(g_{22}-g_{12})/(g_{11}+g_{22}-2g_{12})$ and
$\pi_2^\star=(g_{11}-g_{12})/(g_{11}+g_{22}-2g_{12})$ from the Gram matrix of the two log-loss
gradients. Corollary~3.3 states that the resulting direction has equal inner product with both
gradients, which the authors read as preventing either loss from dominating. This is the
two-vector MGDA rule of Section~\ref{sec:moo_symmetric} applied to log-loss gradients. One
difference is that Eq.~(10) imposes only $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$ while Eq.~(9) asks for the simplex, so
the closed form returns a negative weight whenever $g_{12}>g_{22}$ or $g_{12}>g_{11}$, the case MGDA
handles by clipping. The ideal rule
needs two backward passes. The practical algorithm amortises the weights with a gradient step
on $\pi$ followed by softmax renormalisation, and it adds a feature projection and separate
distillation and task heads. The deployed method is therefore not the closed form, and a
plain-logit adaptation would need to be specified separately. On CIFAR-100,
ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4, DTO-KD reports 76.40\% against 75.44\% for DKD and
73.33\% for KD in the same table. It also reports gains on COCO detection with ViDT students.
Its convergence claim is borrowed from FAMO's analysis rather than proved for the amortised
recipe, and no code is linked from the proceedings page. Compared with AE-KD it balances task and
teacher rather than teachers, and it uses log losses, which removes sensitivity to the scale of
each loss. MoKD \citep{hayder2025mokd}, an earlier preprint by the same first four authors, has
been withdrawn and is superseded by DTO-KD.

\paragraph{SCKD: gradient-similarity gating (lineage).}
SCKD \citep{zhu2021sckd} keeps the supervised loss as an ungated term and switches each
output- or feature-distillation term on or off with a binary gate. The gate uses the cosine
between the supervised gradient and that distillation term's gradient. This makes the label the
primary objective, as the priority rules of Section~\ref{sec:moo_priority} do, but the update is
the plain sum of the surviving losses, not a constrained program. The signal is the student's
optimisation state, not the teacher's correctness. The published rule contradicts the paper's own
explanation. Section~3.4 and Algorithm~1 remove a term when its cosine is
\emph{greater than or equal to} the threshold $\varphi$, with $\varphi=0$ by default, while the
surrounding text speaks of removing conflicting, that is negatively aligned, terms. Algorithm~1
also initialises the gates once, before the training loop, so whether a removed term can return
is not stated. The parameter subset on which the cosine is computed is likewise unspecified. We
found no authoritative code to settle these points. SCKD costs one extra backward pass per
distillation term. Gradient similarity alone does not show that the teacher is reliable, and the
paper does not prove better generalisation.
% zhu2021sckd Eq. (6) (L_SCKD = sum gamma L_kd + H(p^S, y)); Sec. 3.4 "If cos(.) is greater or equal
% than some threshold phi, we regard such KD loss have negative transfer"; Alg. 1 ("Initialize all
% gamma = 1" before the loop; "if cos >= phi then gamma_i = 0"); "we trivially set phi = 0".

Gradient-similarity gating predates SCKD. \citet{du2018auxgradsim} (preprint) weight or admit an
auxiliary loss by its cosine with the main-loss gradient, including a binary admission variant.
The main loss supplies the reference direction, so this is the same asymmetric idea outside
distillation. It does not show improved final generalisation for distillation.

\paragraph{SCOPE: classification-priority conflict removal.}
SCOPE \citep{chen2026scope} targets medical image classification. It combines two components.
Selective Relation Alignment weights pairwise teacher relations by entropy-derived confidence.
Gradient Conflict Resolution removes the parts of the distillation gradient that conflict with
the classification gradient. Classification is the reference objective and is not itself
modified, which makes SCOPE a one-sided projection, the asymmetric counterpart of PCGrad. Like
SCKD it treats the label as primary, but it edits the conflicting component instead of dropping
the whole term. Its full system also contains auxiliary priors and relational losses.
It was published in the Journal of
Imaging and evaluated on medical image classification.

\paragraph{TPKD: preserving task margins (preprint).}
TPKD \citep{yuan2026tpkd} (preprint) asks what part of the teacher's distribution a student
should copy. The authors prove that full imitation can force a student to give back
correct-class margin it already has. They split the teacher signal into a label part and a
conditional part (the teacher's relative probabilities among wrong classes). TPKD keeps the
label gradient intact and projects the conditional gradient onto a cone of output-space
directions that do not shrink any correct-class margin. The projection works on class
probabilities and costs $O(K\log K)$ per example, with no extra backward pass. Unlike SCOPE, which
removes conflict in parameter space, TPKD protects a stated output-space quantity, and the
transfer of that guarantee to parameter updates holds only under the paper's conditions. With a
VOLO-D2 teacher and a pretrained PiT-B student on CIFAR-100 (60 epochs), TPKD reaches
88.05\% against 87.58\% for KD and 87.81\% for DTO-KD over three seeds. The projection's own
contribution is small: without it accuracy is 87.83\% on CIFAR-100, and on CLINC150 the variant
without projection scores slightly higher (93.83\% vs 93.81\%). The benefit comes mostly from
learning the conditional part, not from conflict removal.

\paragraph{PCD as a reference priority rule.}
PCD \citep{varam2026pcd} is the priority rule of Section~\ref{sec:moo_priority} with
per-objective normalisation. It has not been evaluated on distillation, so it enters this section
only as a reference point. Read as a distillation rule, it would keep the label gradient unless
the teacher gradient's progress falls below $\tau$ times its own squared norm. It would then add
the smallest teacher component that restores that progress. This puts it between SCKD, which
drops the teacher term entirely, and SCOPE, which removes the conflicting part of the teacher
gradient. The same caveats as in Section~\ref{sec:moo_priority} apply: priority means proximity
to the label gradient, not a guarantee that the label loss descends, and the method has no
convergence theorem. Its asymmetric anchored projection has antecedents in DBGD, PNGD and
AuxiNash, which the PCD paper acknowledges for DBGD
\citep{gong2021dbgd,ye2022pngd,shamsian2023auxinash}.

\paragraph{DOT and DeepKD: separate momentum.}
DOT \citep{zhao2023dot} changes the optimiser rather than the loss. It keeps two momentum
buffers, one for the cross-entropy gradient with coefficient $\mu-\Delta$ and one for the
distillation gradient with $\mu+\Delta$, and steps along their sum. The larger momentum makes
distillation dominate the trajectory. The rule is asymmetric, but the favoured objective is the
teacher, the reverse of SCKD and PCD. DOT needs two backward passes per step, or one per loss.
On ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4 it lifts KD from 73.33\% to 75.12\%. The authors
compare it with retuning: the best single momentum gives 73.66\%, and the best fixed task weight
gives 73.56\%. DeepKD \citep{huang2025deepkd} extends DOT to three momentum buffers, for the
task, target-class and non-target-class gradients, with coefficients set by each component's
gradient signal-to-noise ratio. It adds a dynamic top-$k$ mask on non-target logits. Its
CIFAR-100 table reports 77.03\% for KD+DeepKD on the same pair. The same table also reports
DKD+DOT at 76.03\%, below DKD alone at 76.32\%. DOT's gain on one method therefore did not
reproduce in DeepKD's runs.

\paragraph{MEMA: history-aware per-sample damping (preprint).}
SPOFA \citep{yang2026spofa} (under review) distils between heterogeneous architectures and adds a
momentum-driven EMA (MEMA) scaler. For each sample it measures the cosine between the task and
distillation gradients in logit space, $S_{t,j}$, and compares it with an EMA baseline $B_{t-1}$.
The distillation weight becomes $\mathrm{clamp}(1-\kappa\max(0,B_{t-1}-S_{t,j}),\omega_{\min},
\omega_{\max})$. Only conflict worse than the running history reduces the weight, so the label
again acts as the reference. Because the probe works in logit space it needs no extra backward
pass. It is a softer, cheaper version of SCKD's gate. On CIFAR-100 with a Swin-T teacher and
ResNet18 student, MEMA alone raises the OFA baseline from 80.54\% to 81.80\%, and the full method
reaches 82.39\%.

\paragraph{CHAD: sample-level usefulness (preprint).}
CHAD \citep{sumit2026chad} (preprint) works per sample. It computes the alignment between each
sample's distillation gradient and the mean validation-loss gradient on a probe subset. It
trains a gate on those labels and uses it to set per-sample distillation weights. The signal
estimates usefulness to the student, not teacher confidence. The authors' own ablation shows a
confidence-only weighting degrading ROUGE-L sharply. On BanSum they report that standard KD
improves ROUGE-L by only $+0.0003$ over cross-entropy. They estimate that 51.3\% of training
samples raise validation loss, and CHAD adds $+0.0173$ over standard KD. The alignment is a
local proxy, not a retraining test of each sample's harm. The multilingual extension evaluates
EWAD+CPDP, not CHAD.

% (probe subset of 5,000, alignment with mean validation gradient); Table 9 (Confidence Only R-L .1169).

Two further 2025--2026 works use MOO elsewhere in the pipeline. \citet{dong2025mooadv} use
Tchebycheff scalarisation to generate diverse adversarial examples for robust distillation from
vision-language models, and UP-MOPD \citep{zhu2026upmopd} (preprint) applies PCGrad-style
projection to per-teacher updates in multi-teacher on-policy LLM distillation. Both are covered
in Section~\ref{sec:emerging}.

\subsection{A critical view}
\label{sec:kd_moo_critical}

The methods above share a premise: the task and distillation gradients conflict often enough,
and badly enough, that the combination rule matters. Few papers test that premise directly.

\paragraph{Symmetric versus priority rules.}
Table~\ref{tab:kd_moo} splits into rules that balance the two losses (AE-KD, DTO-KD) and rules
that rank them. The rankers disagree about which side should win. SCKD, SCOPE, TPKD, MEMA and PCD
protect the label. DOT and DeepKD let distillation lead. Both camps report gains on CIFAR-100,
and no paper compares a label-first rule with a teacher-first rule under one protocol. The
question of which side should have priority in distillation is still untested.

\paragraph{The scalarisation debate.}
In multi-task learning, three large studies found that the specialised optimisers rarely beat a
tuned weighted sum. \citet{xin2022mtohelp} ran large-scale language and vision experiments and
report that these methods ``do not yield any performance improvements beyond what is achievable
via traditional optimization approaches''. \citet{kurin2022unitary} show that unitary
scalarisation with standard regularisation matches or improves on complex multi-task optimisers,
and they interpret several of those optimisers as forms of regularisation. \citet{royer2023scalarization}
revisit the question at larger scale and propose population-based search over the scalarisation
weights instead of new optimisers.
None of these studies covers distillation. Of the distillation papers above, DOT is the one we
found that compares with a sweep of fixed task weights, and every weight in its sweep stays
below DOT. The others we read compare with published baselines at their default weights.

\paragraph{Scale or conflict?}
Some of these gains may come from normalisation and scale, not from resolving conflict.
DTO-KD works on log losses, whose gradients $\nabla L_i/L_i$ already remove each loss's scale.
PCD's authors make the point explicitly for their largest
pruning gains. The raw regulariser gradients were one to two orders of magnitude larger than the
task gradient, and the task--regulariser cosine never exceeded $1.2\times10^{-3}$, so ``the cause
is objective scale rather than gradient conflict'' \citep{varam2026pcd}. They also note that
their weighted-sum grid floored the effective weight far above its best range, which overstates
the gap. TPKD's ablation points the same way: removing the projection costs 0.22 points on
CIFAR-100 and nothing on CLINC150. MEMA is the counter-example: in SPOFA's ablation the
conflict-damping scaler alone adds 1.26 points, more than the LayerNorm projector's 0.65, so
conflict handling can matter in heterogeneous distillation.

\paragraph{Is there measured conflict in distillation?}
We found one direct measurement of CE--KD conflict in parameter space. DHO \citep{kang2025dho}
trains a ResNet-18 student from a zero-shot CLIP ResNet-50 teacher with 16 labelled examples
per class. With a single head, the final-epoch cosine between the two gradients, averaged over
eight datasets, is $-0.39$ at the first backbone stage, $-0.45$ at the last, and $-0.76$ at the
classifier. DHO removes this conflict with separate CE and KD heads rather than a gradient rule.
That setting differs from the standard full-label CIFAR-100 or ImageNet benchmarks with a
trained CNN teacher, where we found no published measurement. Until such measurements exist,
claims that a distillation method works \emph{because} it resolves gradient conflict are
hypotheses, and a tuned or normalised weighted sum is the baseline every such method should
beat.
