\section{Knowledge Distillation as Multi-Objective Optimization}
\label{sec:kd_moo}

Hinton-style distillation minimises $(1-\lambda)L_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda L_{\mathrm{KD}}$
\citep{hinton2015kd}, which is the linear scalarisation of Section~\ref{sec:moo} with $K=2$.
A training step moves the student along the weighted sum of the supervised and teacher gradients. Two loss gradients conflict when their inner product is negative: their
local first-order preferences point in opposing directions \citep{yu2020pcgrad,zhu2021sckd}.
Conflict is a property of the optimisation state, not of the teacher. A teacher can predict the
correct class with high confidence and still conflict with the label gradient. At temperature 1,
with teacher probabilities $(0.85,0.10,0.05)$, student probabilities $(0.95,0.03,0.02)$ and
label class 1, the cross-entropy logit gradient $p_s-y$ and the forward-KL logit gradient
$p_s-p_t$ have inner product $-0.0077$, a local logit-space calculation, not an experiment or a
claim about parameter space. Section~\ref{sec:reliability} covers the separate question of whether the
teacher is right; this section asks how the gradients are combined.

\subsection{How state-of-the-art distillation uses MOO}
\label{sec:kd_moo_methods}

We group methods by rule: symmetric balancing (MGDA line), label-primary gating and projection, and
optimiser-side rules; Table~\ref{tab:kd_moo} summarises them.

\paragraph{AE-KD: MGDA over teachers (lineage).}
AE-KD \citep{du2020aekd} framed ensemble distillation in gradient space: it solves a soft-margin
MGDA over the per-teacher KD losses (dual box constraints $0\le\alpha_m\le C$, $C\in[1/M,1]$; $C=1$
is MGDA), on student-logit gradients, so it is cheap. The CE term sits outside the MOO step, so it
weighs teachers against each other, not task against teacher; AGRE-KD \citep{kenfack2025agrekd}
uses it as a baseline.
% du2020aekd Eq. (7) (CE + weighted KD), Eq. (9) (primal with slack), Eq. (10) (dual, 0 <= alpha_m <= C),
% Eq. (11) (direction); Fig. 1 right (gradients w.r.t. student logits); Sec. 3.2 "C in [1/M, 1]".

\paragraph{DTO-KD: closed-form min-norm on log losses.}
DTO-KD \citep{hayder2026dtokd} applies FAMO to distillation, maximising the worse of the two
relative improvement rates; the dual picks
$\pi^\star=\arg\min_{\pi}\tfrac12\|J\pi\|^2$ with $J=[\nabla\log L_{\mathrm{distill}},\nabla\log
L_{\mathrm{task}}]$, with closed form
$\pi_1^\star=(g_{22}-g_{12})/(g_{11}+g_{22}-2g_{12})$ and
$\pi_2^\star=(g_{11}-g_{12})/(g_{11}+g_{22}-2g_{12})$. By Corollary~3.3 the direction has equal inner product with both gradients, so neither
loss dominates; this is the two-vector MGDA rule of Section~\ref{sec:moo_symmetric} applied to log-loss gradients. One
difference is that Eq.~(10) imposes only $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$ while Eq.~(9) asks for the simplex, so
the closed form returns a negative weight whenever $g_{12}>g_{22}$ or $g_{12}>g_{11}$, the case MGDA
handles by clipping. The ideal rule needs two backward passes; the practical algorithm amortises
$\pi$ with a gradient step and softmax renormalisation and adds a feature projection and separate
distillation and task heads, so the deployed method is not the closed form, and a plain-logit
adaptation would need to be specified separately. On CIFAR-100,
ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4, DTO-KD reports 76.40\% against 75.44\% for DKD and
73.33\% for KD in the same table. It also reports COCO gains with ViDT students. Its convergence claim is borrowed from FAMO rather
than proved for the amortised recipe, no code is linked, and its log losses remove each loss's
scale. MoKD \citep{hayder2025mokd}, an earlier preprint by the same first four authors, has
been withdrawn and is superseded by DTO-KD.

\paragraph{SCKD: gradient-similarity gating (lineage).}
SCKD \citep{zhu2021sckd} keeps the supervised loss as an ungated term and switches each
output- or feature-distillation term on or off with a binary gate. The gate uses the cosine
between the supervised gradient and that distillation term's gradient. This makes the label the
primary objective, as the priority rules of Section~\ref{sec:moo_priority} do, but the update is
the plain sum of the surviving losses, not a constrained program. The signal is the student's optimisation state, not the teacher's correctness. The published rule
appears inconsistent with the paper's own explanation and should not be silently corrected: Section~3.4 and Algorithm~1 remove a term when its cosine is
\emph{greater than or equal to} the threshold $\varphi$, with $\varphi=0$ by default, while the
surrounding text speaks of removing conflicting, that is negatively aligned, terms. Algorithm~1
initialises the gates once, before the loop, so whether a removed term can return is not stated,
nor is the parameter subset for the cosine, and we found no authoritative code. SCKD costs one extra backward pass per
distillation term. Gradient similarity alone does not show that the teacher is reliable, and the
paper does not prove better generalisation.
% zhu2021sckd Eq. (6) (L_SCKD = sum gamma L_kd + H(p^S, y)); Sec. 3.4 "If cos(.) is greater or equal
% than some threshold phi, we regard such KD loss have negative transfer"; Alg. 1 ("Initialize all
% gamma = 1" before the loop; "if cos >= phi then gamma_i = 0"); "we trivially set phi = 0".

Gradient-similarity gating predates SCKD. \citet{du2018auxgradsim} (preprint) weight or admit an
auxiliary loss by its cosine with the main-loss gradient, including a binary admission variant.
This is the same asymmetric idea outside distillation, with no shown generalisation gain for KD.

\paragraph{SCOPE: classification-priority conflict removal.}
SCOPE \citep{chen2026scope} combines two components. Selective Relation Alignment weights pairwise teacher relations by entropy-derived confidence.
Gradient Conflict Resolution removes the parts of the distillation gradient that conflict with
the classification gradient. Classification is the reference objective and is not itself
modified, which makes SCOPE a one-sided projection, the asymmetric counterpart of PCGrad. Like
SCKD it treats the label as primary, but it edits the conflicting component instead of dropping
the whole term. Its full system adds auxiliary priors and relational losses; it appeared in the Journal of Imaging
and is evaluated on medical image classification.

\paragraph{TPKD: preserving task margins (preprint).}
TPKD \citep{yuan2026tpkd} (preprint) proves that full imitation can force a student to give back
correct-class margin it already has. It splits the teacher signal into a label part and a
conditional part (relative probabilities among wrong classes), keeps the label gradient intact and
projects the conditional gradient onto a cone of output-space
directions that do not shrink any correct-class margin, at $O(K\log K)$ per example and no extra
backward pass. Unlike SCOPE, which
removes conflict in parameter space, TPKD protects a stated output-space quantity, and the
transfer of that guarantee to parameter updates holds only under the paper's conditions. With a
VOLO-D2 teacher and a pretrained PiT-B student on CIFAR-100 (60 epochs), TPKD reaches
88.05\% against 87.58\% for KD and 87.81\% for DTO-KD over three seeds. The projection's own
contribution is small (87.83\% without it; on CLINC150 93.83\% without vs 93.81\% with), so the
benefit comes mostly from learning the conditional part.

% projection 87.83 / 93.83; TPKD 88.05 / 93.81); App. E.1 (VOLO-D2 teacher, PiT-B student).

\paragraph{PCD as a reference priority rule.}
PCD \citep{varam2026pcd}, the normalised priority rule of Section~\ref{sec:moo_priority}, has not
been evaluated on distillation and enters only as a reference point. As a KD rule it would keep the
label gradient unless the teacher gradient's progress fell below $\tau$ times its squared norm, then
add the smallest teacher component restoring it, which places it between SCKD (drop the term) and
SCOPE (remove the conflicting part). Priority means proximity to the label gradient, not guaranteed
label-loss descent, and its anchored projection has antecedents in DBGD, PNGD and AuxiNash
\citep{gong2021dbgd,ye2022pngd,shamsian2023auxinash}.

\paragraph{DOT and DeepKD: separate momentum.}
DOT \citep{zhao2023dot} changes the optimiser: separate momentum buffers for the CE gradient
($\mu-\Delta$) and the distillation gradient ($\mu+\Delta$) let distillation dominate the
trajectory, an asymmetric rule favouring the teacher, the reverse of SCKD and PCD, at one backward
pass per loss. On ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4 it lifts KD from 73.33\% to 75.12\%, against
73.66\% for the best single momentum and 73.56\% for the best fixed task weight. DeepKD
\citep{huang2025deepkd} uses three buffers (task, target-class, non-target-class) with coefficients
from each component's gradient signal-to-noise ratio, plus a dynamic top-$k$ mask on non-target
logits, and reports 77.03\% for KD+DeepKD on the same pair; its table also has DKD+DOT at 76.03\%,
below DKD alone at 76.32\%, so DOT's gain did not reproduce in DeepKD's runs.

\paragraph{MEMA: history-aware per-sample damping (preprint).}
SPOFA \citep{yang2026spofa} (under review), for heterogeneous architectures, adds a momentum-driven
EMA (MEMA) scaler that measures, per sample, the cosine between the task and
distillation gradients in logit space, $S_{t,j}$, and compares it with an EMA baseline $B_{t-1}$.
The distillation weight becomes $\mathrm{clamp}(1-\kappa\max(0,B_{t-1}-S_{t,j}),\omega_{\min},
\omega_{\max})$. Only conflict worse than the running history reduces the weight, with the label as reference and
no extra backward pass: a softer, cheaper SCKD gate. On CIFAR-100 with a Swin-T teacher and
ResNet18 student, MEMA alone raises the OFA baseline from 80.54\% to 81.80\%, and the full method
reaches 82.39\%.

% src: yang2026spofa Table IV (ablation, Swin-T -> ResNet18): OFA 80.54, +MEMA 81.80, full 82.39.

\paragraph{CHAD: sample-level usefulness (preprint).}
CHAD \citep{sumit2026chad} (\S\ref{sec:reliability}) weights samples by the alignment of their
distillation gradient with the mean validation gradient on a probe subset, estimating usefulness
rather than teacher confidence; a confidence-only weighting degrades ROUGE-L sharply in its
ablation. On BanSum, standard KD gains only $+0.0003$ ROUGE-L over cross-entropy, 51.3\% of samples
are estimated to raise validation loss, and CHAD adds $+0.0173$ over KD; the multilingual extension
evaluates EWAD+CPDP, not CHAD.

\paragraph{Learned weighting and distiller search.}
DistPro \citep{deng2022distpro} meta-learns a time-varying weight schedule over a set of KD pathways. Its outer-loop weights follow trends over training but cannot react to conflict at a single step. It reports 77.54 on ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ShuffleNetV2 and 71.89 on ImageNet ResNet-34$\to$ResNet-18.
KDAS \citep{chae2025kdas} adds adaptive sampling (about 10--30\% less training time; 71.81 for DKD+KDAS on ImageNet). Auto-KD \citep{li2023autokd} searches distiller designs by Monte-Carlo tree search (77.61 on ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4 with logit KD, 77.35 without; 72.45 on ImageNet), UniADS \citep{lu2024uniads} searches student and distiller jointly (76.65; 72.25 with a 73.40 teacher). Choosing the combination by learning or search puts these methods nearer the tuned-weight baselines of Section~\ref{sec:kd_moo_critical} than the per-step rules, at the cost of an outer loop.
KD-Zero \citep{li2023kdzero}, the evolutionary counterpart of Auto-KD, searches transforms, distances and loss terms per pair and trains 240 epochs, reaching 77.85\% on ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4 and 72.17\% on ImageNet ResNet34$\to$ResNet18, excluding search cost.

Two further 2025--2026 works use MOO elsewhere in the pipeline. Outside our scope, \citet{dong2025mooadv} use
Tchebycheff scalarisation to generate adversarial examples for robust VLM distillation, and UP-MOPD
\citep{zhu2026upmopd} (preprint) applies PCGrad-style projection to per-teacher updates in on-policy
LLM distillation (\S\ref{sec:emerging}).

\subsection{A critical view}
\label{sec:kd_moo_critical}

These methods assume that task and distillation gradients conflict often and badly enough for the
combination rule to matter; few papers test this directly.

\paragraph{Symmetric versus priority rules.}
Some rules balance the two losses (AE-KD, DTO-KD); the rankers disagree on which side wins. SCKD,
SCOPE, TPKD, MEMA and PCD protect the label, DOT and DeepKD let distillation lead, both camps report
CIFAR-100 gains, and no paper compares them under one protocol, so priority remains an open question.
% INTERPRETATION.

\paragraph{The scalarisation debate.}
In multi-task learning, three large studies found that specialised optimisers rarely beat a tuned
weighted sum. \citet{xin2022mtohelp} report, from large-scale language and vision experiments, that these methods ``do not yield any performance improvements beyond what is achievable
via traditional optimization approaches''. \citet{kurin2022unitary} show that unitary
scalarisation with standard regularisation matches or improves on complex multi-task optimisers,
and they interpret several of those optimisers as forms of regularisation. \citet{royer2023scalarization}
revisit it at larger scale and propose population-based search over the weights instead of new
optimisers. None of these covers distillation. Among the KD papers above, only DOT compares with a
sweep of fixed task weights (all below DOT); the others we read use published baselines at default
weights, and we did not check DistPro, KDAS, Auto-KD or UniADS for such a sweep.

\paragraph{Scale or conflict?}
Some gains may come from normalisation and scale rather than conflict resolution. DTO-KD's
log-loss gradients $\nabla L_i/L_i$ already remove each loss's scale, and PCD's authors say so for
their largest pruning gains: regulariser gradients were one to two orders of magnitude larger than
the task gradient and the task--regulariser cosine never exceeded $1.2\times10^{-3}$, so ``the cause
is objective scale rather than gradient conflict'' \citep{varam2026pcd}. They also note that
their weighted-sum grid floored the effective weight far above its best range, which overstates
the gap. TPKD's ablation points the same way: removing the projection costs 0.22 points on
CIFAR-100 and nothing on CLINC150. MEMA is the counter-example: in SPOFA's ablation the
conflict-damping scaler alone adds 1.26 points, more than the LayerNorm projector's 0.65, so
conflict handling can matter in heterogeneous distillation.

\paragraph{Is there measured conflict in distillation?}
We found one direct measurement of CE--KD conflict in parameter space. DHO \citep{kang2025dho}
trains a ResNet-18 student from a zero-shot CLIP ResNet-50 teacher with 16 labelled examples
per class. With a single head, the final-epoch cosine between the two gradients, averaged over
eight datasets, is $-0.39$ at the first backbone stage, $-0.45$ at the last, and $-0.76$ at the
classifier. DHO removes this conflict with separate CE and KD heads rather than a gradient rule.
We found no such measurement on the standard full-label CIFAR-100 or ImageNet benchmarks. Until
one exists, claims that a distillation method works \emph{because} it resolves gradient conflict are
hypotheses, and a tuned or normalised weighted sum is the baseline every such method should
beat.
