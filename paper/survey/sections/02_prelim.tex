\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

This section fixes the notation that the rest of the survey uses, states the classical distillation
objective and the role of its temperature, and describes the benchmarks on which the methods of
\S\ref{sec:logit} onwards are compared.

\subsection{Notation and the classical objective}
\label{sec:prelim-notation}

We consider $C$-way classification with an input $\mathbf{x}$ and a label $y \in \{1,\dots,C\}$.
A pretrained teacher $f_t$ and a student $f_\theta$ with parameters $\theta$ produce the logit vectors
$\mathbf{z}_t = f_t(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{z}_s = f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ in $\mathbb{R}^C$.
In the standard (offline) setting the teacher is trained first and kept fixed while the student
learns \citep{hinton2015kd}. For a temperature $T>0$ the softened class distribution is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:softmax-T}
  p^{T}_{c}(\mathbf{z}) = \frac{\exp(z_c/T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp(z_j/T)}, \qquad c=1,\dots,C,
\end{equation}
so $\mathbf{p}^{1}(\mathbf{z}_s)$ is the ordinary softmax prediction of the student.
\citet{hinton2015kd} train the student on the ground-truth label and on the teacher's softened
distribution at the same time. Following the original draft of this survey, we write the objective as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kd-objective}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\big(y,\mathbf{p}^{1}(\mathbf{z}_s)\big)}_{\text{task loss}}
  + \lambda\,\underbrace{T^{2}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_t)\,\big\|\,\mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_s)\big)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(y,\mathbf{p}) = -\log p_y$ and $\lambda \ge 0$ sets how much the
distillation term contributes. Other papers write the same objective as a convex combination
$(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + \alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}$, which differs only by a
rescaling of the learning rate. Throughout the survey, ``KD'' with no qualifier means
Eq.~\eqref{eq:kd-objective}, and the methods of \S\ref{sec:logit} are described by what they change
in $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}$, in $\mathbf{p}^{T}$, or in the weight $\lambda$.
Figure~\ref{fig:kd-pipeline} shows the resulting pipeline.

\paragraph{Per-objective gradients.}
Section~\ref{sec:moo} treats Eq.~\eqref{eq:kd-objective} as a two-objective problem, so we name the
two gradients here. With respect to the student logits, the cross-entropy gives the usual
$\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}/\partial\mathbf{z}_s = \mathbf{p}^{1}(\mathbf{z}_s) - \mathbf{e}_y$,
where $\mathbf{e}_y$ is the one-hot label, and \citet{hinton2015kd} show that the softened KL term has
per-logit gradient $(1/T)\big(p^{T}_{c}(\mathbf{z}_s) - p^{T}_{c}(\mathbf{z}_t)\big)$. After the $T^2$
factor this gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:logit-grads}
  \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_s} = T\big(\mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_s) - \mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_t)\big).
\end{equation}
Writing $\mathbf{J}_\theta = \partial\mathbf{z}_s/\partial\theta$ for the student's Jacobian, the
per-objective parameter gradients are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:param-grads}
  \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{CE}} = \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} = \mathbf{J}_\theta^{\top}\big(\mathbf{p}^{1}(\mathbf{z}_s)-\mathbf{e}_y\big),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KD}} = \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}} = T\,\mathbf{J}_\theta^{\top}\big(\mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_s)-\mathbf{p}^{T}(\mathbf{z}_t)\big),
\end{equation}
and plain gradient descent on Eq.~\eqref{eq:kd-objective} follows $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KD}}$
(in practice averaged over a mini-batch). We say the two objectives \emph{conflict} on a batch when
$\langle\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{CE}},\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KD}}\rangle < 0$, that is, when their cosine
similarity is negative. Equation~\eqref{eq:logit-grads} already shows why this can happen even with a
correct teacher: $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ pushes the student towards the one-hot vector, while
$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KD}}$ pulls it towards a softened teacher distribution that keeps mass on the
non-target classes. Whether such conflicts matter in practice is an empirical question that
\S\ref{sec:moo} and \S\ref{sec:kd_moo} take up.

\subsection{The role of temperature}
\label{sec:prelim-temperature}

The temperature controls how soft the teacher and student distributions are. At $T=1$ a confident
teacher puts almost all of its mass on one class, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}$ carries little more
information than the label. Raising $T$ exposes the relative probabilities that the teacher assigns
to the wrong classes, which \citet{hinton2015kd} call the knowledge the teacher has about which
classes resemble one another. The $T^2$ factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:kd-objective} has a purpose. Because
the soft-target gradients scale as $1/T^2$, multiplying by $T^2$ keeps the relative contribution of the
two terms roughly unchanged when $T$ is tuned \citep{hinton2015kd}. In the high-temperature limit, and
with zero-mean logits, the KD term reduces to matching logits under a squared error
\citep{hinton2015kd}. KD therefore sits between two extremes: label-like targets at low $T$ and raw
logit regression at high $T$.

Three properties of the classical recipe drive much of the work reviewed in \S\ref{sec:logit}.
First, $T$ is a single global hyper-parameter shared by teacher and student and by all samples, and
it is usually chosen by grid search \citep{li2023ctkd}. Second, sharing $T$ between the two networks
implicitly asks the student to reproduce the teacher's logit \emph{range} as well as its ranking of
classes, which a smaller network may not be able to do \citep{sun2024lskd}. Third, the KD term mixes
what the teacher says about the target class with what it says about the other classes, and the
confidence of the teacher on the target class scales the second part down \citep{zhao2022dkd}.
CTKD learns $T$, LSKD standardises the logits before the softmax, and DKD separates the two parts of
the KL term; \S\ref{sec:logit} compares them.

The KD temperature should not be confused with the temperature used for calibration.
\citet{guo2017calibration} show that the softmax confidence of modern networks is often miscalibrated
and fit a single temperature on held-out data after training to correct it. That calibration
temperature is chosen so that confidence matches accuracy; the KD temperature is chosen so that the
student learns better. A teacher distilled at a large $T$ is not therefore a calibrated teacher, and
a calibrated teacher is not necessarily a good one to distil from.

\subsection{Benchmarks, teacher--student pairs and metrics}
\label{sec:prelim-benchmarks}

Most logit-distillation papers since 2022 report results on the same three datasets, using the
protocol and code base that CRD \citep{tian2020crd} introduced for CIFAR-100 and that DKD's
\texttt{mdistiller} repository \citep{zhao2022dkd} extended to ImageNet and COCO.
\emph{CIFAR-100} \citep{krizhevsky2009cifar} has 100 classes of $32\times32$ images, with 50k
training and 10k validation images; \emph{ImageNet-1K} \citep{russakovsky2015imagenet} has 1{,}000
classes with about 1.28M training and 50k validation images; and \emph{MS-COCO}
\citep{lin2014coco} is used for 80-class detection with the 118k-image \texttt{train2017} and
5k-image \texttt{val2017} splits (split sizes as reported by \citet{zhao2022dkd}, Sec.~4).
% src: DKD Sec. 4 (CIFAR-100 50k/10k; ImageNet 1.28M/50k; COCO train2017 118k, val2017 5k)

On CIFAR-100, papers distinguish homogeneous pairs, in which teacher and student come from the same
family (ResNet56$\to$ResNet20, ResNet110$\to$ResNet32, ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4,
WRN-40-2$\to$WRN-16-2, WRN-40-2$\to$WRN-40-1, VGG13$\to$VGG8), from heterogeneous pairs
(ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ShuffleNet-V1/V2, ResNet50$\to$MobileNetV2, VGG13$\to$MobileNetV2,
WRN-40-2$\to$ShuffleNet-V1). On ImageNet the standard pairs are ResNet34$\to$ResNet18 and
ResNet50$\to$MobileNet \citep{zhao2022dkd,yang2023nkd}. ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4 has
become the most quoted single number: in this protocol the teacher reaches 79.42\%, the student trained
alone 72.50\%, and classical KD 73.33\% top-1 \citep[Table~6]{zhao2022dkd}. We use this pair for
Table~\ref{tab:logit-sota}.
% src: DKD Table 6 (teacher 79.42, student 72.50, KD 73.33; average of 5 trials)

The headline metric is top-1 accuracy on the validation set, on CIFAR-100 usually averaged over a few
runs (five in DKD, \citealp{zhao2022dkd}); ImageNet papers add top-5 accuracy, and detection papers
report COCO mAP together with AP$_{50}$ and AP$_{75}$ \citep{li2023ctkd}. Newer papers also report
expected calibration error \citep{hossain2025luminet} and training cost, such as per-batch latency and
extra parameters \citep{hossain2025luminet}, because a gain in accuracy that triples training time is
a different trade-off from a free one.

Two cautions apply to every table in this survey. First, papers do not reuse each other's numbers
consistently. On ResNet32$\times$4$\to$ResNet8$\times$4, CTKD is listed at 73.39\% by
\citet{sun2024lskd} but at 73.70\% by \citet{xu2025ldrld}, and LSKD reports 76.62\% for itself while
\citet{xu2025ldrld} list it at 76.16\%. Gaps of 0.3--0.5 points are larger than several of the differences between the 2023--2025 methods in Table~\ref{tab:logit-sota}. Second, \citet{miles2024vkd} argue that CIFAR-100 is increasingly saturated as a KD
benchmark, so some of the reported gains may come from regularisation rather than from better
transfer of knowledge (\textbf{VERIFIED} as their claim; whether it holds for a given method is open).
We therefore report each method's own number with its source table and do not rank methods whose
gaps are within this spread.
