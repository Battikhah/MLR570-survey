\section{Emerging Settings}
\label{sec:emerging}

\paragraph{LLM distillation.} Sequence generation makes the student’s own state distribution part of the training problem. MiniLLM optimizes reverse KL on student-generated sequences, while GKD trains on student samples with teacher feedback under a selectable divergence; both address the mismatch between teacher-forced targets and errors the student actually generates. MiniLLM's reverse-KL objective is directed at the student's generated support, while GKD lets the user choose a divergence and uses generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence in its main formulation; either approach requires teacher feedback on student outputs, which raises sampling and teacher-inference cost relative to offline logit matching \citep{gu2024minillm,agarwal2024gkd}. DistiLLM reduces that cost through skew KL and adaptive off-policy replay, while DistiLLM-2 adds a contrastive objective over teacher and student responses; these are alternatives to GKD’s fully on-policy formulation, though replay can be less current than on-policy samples and contrastive training depends on response pairs. DistiLLM is useful when training throughput constrains fully on-policy updates; GKD is a clearer choice when student-generated error coverage is central, while DistiLLM-2 targets cases where teacher and student responses can be contrasted \citep{ko2024distillm,ko2025distillm2}. These methods extend the logit-divergence discussion in §\ref{sec:logit}, while CaRE-Div connects LLM distillation to selective transfer in §\ref{sec:reliability} and UP-MOPD applies update projection to multi-teacher on-policy distillation in §\ref{sec:kd_moo} \citep{sengupta2026casd,zhu2026upmopd}. The comparison also separates objective choice from data collection: MiniLLM and GKD change how teacher signals are applied to generated continuations, while DistiLLM and DistiLLM-2 address efficiency or response contrast. They should be compared on generation quality and training budget together, since an objective advantage alone does not show lower end-to-end distillation cost \citep{gu2024minillm,agarwal2024gkd,ko2024distillm,ko2025distillm2}.

\paragraph{Vision-language teachers and small students.} TinyCLIP transfers cross-modal affinity structure and inherits teacher weights, whereas CLIP-KD's empirical comparison finds simple feature mimicry to be a strong baseline; the former offers a compression recipe, while the latter gives a broader comparison of CLIP distillation objectives \citep{wu2023tinyclip,yang2024clipkd}. DHO adapts VLM supervision to semi-supervised classification with separate classification and distillation heads, explicitly measuring conflict between the objectives; this links its design to feature and representation transfer in §\ref{sec:feature} and gradient handling in §\ref{sec:kd_moo} \citep{kang2025dho}. CLIP-KD and TinyCLIP focus on compressing a vision-language model, while DHO uses VLMs as teachers when labeled data are scarce; the latter is less directly applicable when a large paired image-text corpus is available for ordinary CLIP compression. TinyCLIP's weight inheritance is useful when teacher and student architectures permit reuse, whereas CLIP-KD's study is useful for selecting among candidate transfer losses; DHO instead addresses limited-label classification and should not be treated as a direct CLIP-compression benchmark \citep{yang2024clipkd,wu2023tinyclip,kang2025dho}.

\paragraph{Adversarially robust distillation.} ARD and RSLAD establish the earlier robust-teacher and robust-soft-label lineage, while recent methods address different weaknesses in transfer: AdaAD adaptively searches for adversarial inputs where student and teacher disagree, PeerAiD trains a specialized peer on the student's adversaries, and SAAD weights examples by transfer consistency \citep{goldblum2020ard,zi2021rslad,huang2023adaad,jung2024peeraid,lee2025saad}. AdaAD changes the adversarial examples used during teacher guidance, PeerAiD changes the tutor, and SAAD changes each sample's weight; the mechanisms suit different sources of mismatch and are not directly interchangeable. CIARD iteratively aligns clean and robust teachers, making teacher conflict explicit, whereas the other methods primarily adapt adversarial examples, tutor choice, or per-sample transfer. ARD and RSLAD remain useful baselines for teacher-guided robust training and robust soft targets; AdaAD fits cases where teacher-student disagreement should shape adversary generation, PeerAiD where a fixed teacher poorly matches student adversaries, and SAAD where consistency varies across samples. These design choices change training cost and supervision source, so reported robustness should be compared only under matched threat models and evaluation protocols \citep{goldblum2020ard,zi2021rslad,huang2023adaad,jung2024peeraid,lee2025saad}. This setting meets the reliability question in §\ref{sec:reliability}: a strong teacher need not transfer useful robustness uniformly. The related CA-AT work projects adversarial gradients relative to clean gradients, connecting this setting to the gradient-conflict discussion in §\ref{sec:kd_moo}; however, the registry has only an unverified arXiv record and a BMVC venue lead, so it is cited as an unverified preprint rather than established venue evidence \citep{xue2024caat} (preprint, unverified).

\paragraph{Federated distillation.} Federated KD must transfer across clients without assuming a shared private training set. DFRD performs server-side data-free distillation using a conditional generator and an exponential moving average, while FedGO weights client logits with a discriminator and gives a near-optimality guarantee; DFRD targets heterogeneous-client forgetting, whereas FedGO addresses ensemble weighting \citep{luo2023dfrd,jang2025fedgo}. HYDRA-FL adds shallow-layer distillation to reduce poisoning amplification, and FedRevive uses data-free distillation to refresh stale asynchronous updates; the first is aimed at robustness under attack, the second at client staleness \citep{khan2024hydrafl,askin2026fedrevive}. These methods extend the data-free and multi-teacher schemes in §\ref{sec:schemes}, and their logit transfer connects to §\ref{sec:logit}; their privacy and communication properties depend on the particular server data, generator, and message protocol, so the presence of KD alone does not establish a privacy guarantee. DFRD is relevant when client heterogeneity and server-side data scarcity dominate; FedGO when combining client predictions is the main challenge; HYDRA-FL when poisoning risk is part of the threat model; and FedRevive when asynchronous delay makes updates stale. Their objectives differ, and the reported accuracy or robustness evidence in one method does not transfer automatically to another federated protocol \citep{luo2023dfrd,jang2025fedgo,khan2024hydrafl,askin2026fedrevive}.

Diffusion-model distillation is outside this survey's scope.
